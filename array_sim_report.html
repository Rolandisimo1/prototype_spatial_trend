Do array-level camera estimators detect population trends?

Simulation comparison of camera-level occupancy, array-level occupancy, and array-level Royle–Nichols · 540 fits, 30 replicates per design cell

Summary

We simulated 540 datasets to ask whether aggregating camera detections to the array level, or switching to a Royle–Nichols abundance-driven detection model at that level, improves our ability to detect a temporal trend when camera data are integrated with iNaturalist records. The design crossed three camera estimators with three abundance levels and two trend scenarios, at 30 replicates each. All 540 tasks completed, every design cell holds exactly 30 replicates, and the degeneracy guard clears, so the harness behaved.

No estimator detects the global trend. Power never exceeds 0.167 in any arm at any abundance, and it does not increase with abundance. The camera-level baseline performs no better than the array arms, which is itself the most useful result: aggregating to arrays costs no measurable power. Array Royle–Nichols is the best-calibrated arm and array occupancy is clearly the worst.

Two corrections before the numbers are read

1. The "null" scenario is not a no-trend scenario

The driver builds it by setting the regional amplitude to zero, which removes among-region deviations while leaving the global trend untouched. We confirmed the true total trend is −0.1795 in every one of the 540 rows, in both scenarios. The detection column under "null" is therefore power measured against a real trend, not a false-positive rate.

A Fisher exact test on the two columns confirms they measure the same quantity:

Any statement of the form "estimator X has a Y% false-positive rate" from this run would be wrong. There is no false-positive rate in this design, and the apparent miscalibration flagged during aggregation dissolves.

2. The design cannot detect an effect of this size

The true trend is −0.18 and mean credible-interval widths are 1.0 to 5.1. To exclude zero an interval would need a half-width below 0.18, so intervals are 2.7× too wide for Royle–Nichols, 5.0× for camera-level, and 14.3× for array occupancy. Power near zero is a property of the reduced simulation design, not evidence that these estimators cannot work.

Array occupancy is poorly behaved

Its intervals are three to five times wider than the alternatives, its bias reaches −0.41 at bobcat-like abundance, and its spatial field error is 16 to 17 against 1.8 to 3.2 for the other arms. Coverage is nominal or above, but that is coverage bought with an interval too wide to be useful. The pattern holds at all three abundance levels, so it is not sampling noise.

Array Royle–Nichols is the best-calibrated arm

It produces the narrowest intervals (0.96 to 1.05), the smallest bias (+0.007 to +0.109), and coverage between 0.87 and 0.97. If any array-level estimator merits further work it is Royle–Nichols rather than occupancy, which inverts our prior expectation that occupancy would be the conservative choice.

Spatial discrimination survives where trend detection fails

AUC is 0.63 to 0.70 across all arms and is not distinguishable among them, while camera-level retains the lowest spatial field error. This reproduces the power-versus-discrimination split found in the lab's earlier camera-versus-array comparison: aggregation can preserve the ability to say where while degrading the ability to say whether.

The robustness indicator never fires

It is exactly zero in all 540 simulations. This is consistent with no arm resolving the trend, and it matches the real moose fits, where the same indicator sits at 0.24 to 0.26 against its 0.5 threshold. The simulation reproduces the failure mode we observe in real data.

Comparison with the camera-level sweep

The two sweeps share no metric columns

We verified this directly. They also fit different models: the earlier sweep fitted the ecoregion model and scored per-region trend sign recovery, whereas the present sweep fits global-scalar models only and scores whether the global trend's interval excludes zero.

This sweep therefore does not show that array aggregation destroyed the 66% sign recovery, because it never measured sign recovery. Both findings stand together: camera-level data can identify which direction a region is moving better than chance, and no estimator here can establish that the global trend differs from zero.

The one directly comparable statement is the most useful one. Within this sweep, on identical simulated data, the camera-level arm performs no better than the array arms, so array aggregation costs nothing measurable. The concern carried over from the earlier camera-versus-array comparison, that aggregation would reduce power, is not supported for Royle–Nichols.

What to resolve before ranking estimators

  1. Add sign recovery to the collector. It is the metric that made the earlier sweep informative and the only way to make the two genuinely comparable. This is inexpensive.
  2. Diagnose array occupancy's interval inflation. The mean detection proportion is 0.13 to 0.15, so the graded-replicate structure is present as designed, and the array count is a constant 97 across all cells — worth confirming that 97 is the intended reduced-design size.
  3. Establish whether −0.18 is detectable under this design at all. If not, power should be dropped as a discriminating metric and replaced with sign recovery and calibration.

We recommend against using this run to select an estimator. It supports three conclusions — array occupancy is poorly behaved, Royle–Nichols is well calibrated, and array aggregation costs no measurable power — and the metric mismatch should be repaired before any ranking is attempted.

Limitations

These are correctly-specified simulations: data were generated by the same structure that was then fitted, so performance on real data will be worse. The Royle–Nichols arm's known weakness, unmodelled heterogeneity in detection within an array, is absent by construction, so its performance here is optimistic. Finally, a rate estimated from 30 replicates carries a 95% interval of roughly ±0.18, and most arm-to-arm differences reported above are smaller than that; the level of power is resolvable, the ranking among arms mostly is not.
