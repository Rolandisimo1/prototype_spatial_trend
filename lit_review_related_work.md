# Related work for a continental, camera-anchored mammal trend analysis

*Literature review prepared 10 October 2026. Searches run against OpenAlex (383 candidate works retained after screening), supplemented by targeted web searches for grey literature. Full bibliography with abstracts in `lit_review_bibliography.csv`.*

## 1. Scope

We estimate population trends for a fleet of North American mammals by fitting camera-trap detections and iNaturalist records to one shared surface, and reporting the camera-anchored component. Four bodies of work bear on this: large-scale trend estimation from semi-structured citizen science, which is almost entirely avian; camera-trap monitoring networks and what they report; the statistical literature on integrating data types; and the state agency products against which any North American mammal trend will be read. We also searched specifically for prior use of iNaturalist to estimate trends, and for integration work in other taxa.

## 2. The bird precedent

Bird monitoring is where continental trend estimation from mixed data is most mature, and it supplies both our template and the standard our results will be judged against. [Rosenberg et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaw1313) estimated a net loss of nearly three billion birds in North America since 1970 by combining breeding survey data with independent radar measures of migration biomass, demonstrating that a continental trend claim can carry policy weight. The underlying program, [the North American Breeding Bird Survey](https://doi.org/10.5066/f7c24tnp), remains the reference standard for structured avian trend data.

The eBird Status and Trends program is the direct analogue to what we attempt. [Fink et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1002/eap.2056) model full annual cycle distribution and population trends from semi-structured citizen science, using participant-reported effort covariates (duration, distance, protocol) to control the observation process. The critical difference from our setting is that eBird checklists are semi-structured: observers record effort and submit complete checklists, which makes a detection-nondetection likelihood available. iNaturalist records carry no effort field, which is why our iNaturalist likelihood is a count model with an effort offset built from total mammal records rather than a checklist model.

Two papers address reliability directly and should be cited where we justify anchoring on cameras. [Feng et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0257226) compare relative abundance indices from standardized surveys against citizen science for the same species, and [Boersch‐Supan et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biocon.2019.108286) test how robust simple trend models are to semi-structured citizen science data. [Johnston et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adn4381) is the most recent large-scale result, finding that North American bird declines are steepest where species are most abundant. That pattern is testable in our fleet, which spans abundant and sparse species on a common footprint.

## 3. Camera-trap trend monitoring

Camera trapping is well established for occupancy and density, and much less so for multi-species continental trends. [Burton et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/1365-2664.12432) is the standard methodological review, and [Steenweg et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1002/fee.1448) argues for scaling camera traps into coordinated networks, which is the design our fleet follows. [Rich et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/geb.12600) is the closest published precedent for continental multi-species inference, assessing global patterns in carnivore occupancy and richness by integrating many surveys.

On what camera data can support, [Sollmann et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biocon.2012.12.025) deserves engagement rather than a passing citation: relative abundance indices from camera trapping are risky because detection and abundance are confounded, which is the problem our Royle-Nichols comparison addresses directly. [Chandler et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1214/12-aoas610) give the spatially explicit alternative for unmarked populations. For infrastructure, [Ahumada et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0376892919000298) describe Wildlife Insights, the platform our detections come through.

The TEAM network produced the main prior example of camera-trap trend estimation across many species, reporting occupancy dynamics for 13 mammal species over five years at a Costa Rican site and deriving a Wildlife Picture Index. That is the methodological ancestor of our work, at one site rather than a continent.

**Grey literature.** Snapshot Global runs standardized camera-trap monitoring in the United States, Japan, Europe, Paraguay, Brazil and Chile. Snapshot Chile is the most relevant national example: CONAF runs annual monitoring in 35 protected areas spanning every region of Chile, conducted by park rangers, with data moving into the new Biodiversity and Protected Areas Service under Law 21.600. A camera-trap assessment of seven protected areas in Magallanes (278 cameras, 9,936 trap-days, 2015-2022) reports detection rates per area and concludes that generalist monitoring designs work for common species but not rare ones. That conclusion matches our own result that camera-only fits cannot resolve a trend for low-detection species.

## 4. iNaturalist for trends: the gap

We searched specifically for prior use of iNaturalist to estimate population trends. The literature is close to empty, and what exists is cautionary.

Only 137 works in OpenAlex match "iNaturalist" and "trend" in title or abstract, and almost none estimate a population trend. [Soberón et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.17161/bi.v20i1.24266) is the one paper directly on the question. It is theoretical rather than empirical: two metrics (records per unit effort, species per unit effort) under two models (a fisheries catch-per-effort model and the Michaelis-Menten equation), illustrated with Mexican butterflies. Three results bear on our design. Species-richness per unit effort trends artifactually negative as effort grows, so richness is the metric to avoid. Abundance per unit effort is the safer choice, which is the metric we use. And a probability argument shows that when population size greatly exceeds the number of records, iNaturalist record counts approximate the number of distinct individuals sighted rather than repeat sightings of a few, which supports treating records as an abundance index.

The paper also describes the opposite data regime from ours, and the contrast is worth drawing explicitly. Soberon and Christen rule out occupancy models for their setting because the data available in many tropical countries lack the dense sampling and true absences those models require. Our camera-trap stream supplies exactly what they lack. Their analysis is what can be done when only opportunistic records exist; ours is what becomes possible when a structured stream is available to anchor them.

One caution from that paper applies directly to us, and we had already tested it. Soberon and Christen note that an effort measure built from record counts becomes circular when records are also the response, "dividing a quantity by the same quantity." Our effort offset is total mammal records per cell-year while the response is a single species' records in that cell-year, so the denominator does contain the numerator. Three results show this is not driving our estimates. First, the contribution of any one species to the pool is bounded: white-tailed deer is the largest at 9.7% of all mammal records and no species exceeds 10%. Second, and decisively, circularity predicts that the species dominating the denominator should be dragged downward hardest, and the opposite holds. Across the fleet 19 of 35 species' shares decline and 16 rise (median -0.98%/yr), but all five of the largest contributors are gaining share: white-tailed deer +4.6%/yr, eastern gray squirrel +8.2%/yr, eastern fox squirrel +6.1%/yr, northern raccoon +2.5%/yr, and mule deer +0.6%/yr (not significant). Third, the model's iNaturalist-side trends track the raw share trends closely (bobcat -3.4%/yr modelled against -2.4%/yr raw; deer +2.7 against +4.6), so the effort control is behaving as intended rather than manufacturing a signal. Total mammal records grew from 2,798 in 2008 to 369,355 in 2025, so this is a demanding test of the correction rather than an easy one.

[Taylor et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1101/794396) show that opportunistic photographs support large-scale phenological trends, the nearest successful precedent. Phenology is a within-year timing question rather than a between-year abundance question, however, so it avoids the effort-drift problem Soberon and Christen identify.

The best-known iNaturalist papers characterize the data rather than estimate trends from it: [Cecco et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/biosci/biab093) on how participants contribute and what that implies, [Mesaglio et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1071/wr20154) on the platform's history and outlook, [Callaghan et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-98584-7) on body-size bias in unstructured citizen science, and [Mason et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/biosci/biaf104) on research uptake. [Feldman et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0234587) review how citizen-science data have been used as input to distribution models and identify the same gap. [Jensen et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1093/jmammal/gyag025) survey iNaturalist mammal data globally, covering status, trends and outlook for macroecology and conservation, and is the natural companion paper to this analysis.

**The most important precedent is [Jachowski et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0254387).** They assessed three North American weasel species using four data streams: historical harvest records, museum collections, iNaturalist observations, and a US-wide trail camera survey, reporting 87-94% declines in harvest over 60 years. This is the closest existing work in data, taxa and geography. The difference is methodological. They compare streams descriptively and triangulate; we fit them jointly and report the camera-anchored component. Our contribution is best framed against this paper.

## 5. Data integration: the methodological lineage

Three reviews define the subfield and should anchor our Methods. [Miller et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210x.13110) survey data integration methods for estimating distributions, [Fletcher et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1002/ecy.2710) give the practical guide to combining data types, and [Isaac et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tree.2019.08.006) set out the case for integration in large-scale distribution models. [Zipkin et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1002/fee.2290) extend the framing to linking processes across scales, and [Isaac et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210x.12254) is the earlier statement of the general problem of extracting change from noisy unstructured data.

The trend-specific subset is much smaller, and that is where our paper sits. [Zipkin et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/ecy.1831) integrate count and detection-nondetection data to model population dynamics, structurally the closest published model to ours. [Pagel et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210x.12221) quantify range-wide variation in population trends by combining local abundance surveys with widespread opportunistic occurrence records: the same two-stream structure, a decade earlier and without a shared spatial surface. [Grüss et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/icesjms/fsz075) develop spatio-temporal models from multiple data types for evaluating population trends in a marine setting. [Doser et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210x.13811) provide integrated community occupancy models for multi-species assessment.

[Maes et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/bij.12530) is worth citing for the applied stakes, since opportunistic data already inform IUCN Red List assessments.

## 6. Butterflies and other taxa

Butterflies are the one invertebrate group with trend infrastructure comparable to birds, and the integration work transfers directly. [Dennis et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/cobi.12956) use citizen science butterfly counts to predict species population trends, combining structured transect counts with less structured records, which is the closest non-avian analogue to our design. [Wepprich et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0216270) report butterfly abundance declines over 20 years of systematic monitoring in Ohio, and [Warren et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2002551117) synthesize the European decline. Together they show how a well-structured volunteer scheme performs, which is the benchmark our less structured inputs are measured against.

## 7. State agency products

Any continental mammal trend will be read against what state wildlife agencies publish, and those products are almost entirely harvest-based.

Montana FWP derives furbearer population trends from catch-per-unit-effort in its annual Furbearer Harvest Survey, and states for several species that the level of harvest and population trend is widely unknown because no license is required. Iowa DNR monitors furbearer trends through three instruments, annual furharvest reports, a spring spotlight survey and a bowhunter observation survey, and notes that harvest data may only indicate a trend in abundance. The Pennsylvania Game Commission has run a furtaker survey and a game-warden questionnaire since 1990 and 1995. Colorado Parks and Wildlife, Oklahoma ODWC and the North Carolina Wildlife Resources Commission publish comparable products.

Two features matter for our framing. First, harvest indices confound population change with effort change, and Iowa's and Montana's own reports attribute harvest declines partly to fewer trappers and shifting pelt prices. That is the same effort-confounding problem we face on the iNaturalist side, with the same structure and no correction applied. Second, coverage is uneven and biased toward furbearers and game species, so a method producing trends for non-game species on a common footprint fills a real gap. [Scheele et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biocon.2019.04.023) make the general version of this point: continental assessment reveals monitoring inadequate for most threatened vertebrates.

## 8. Where this study sits

Four claims are defensible from this review.

1. No published study estimates population trends for a mammal fleet from iNaturalist records. The one paper directly on the question cautions against doing it naively, and the closest applied work treats iNaturalist as one descriptive stream among four.
2. The integration literature is overwhelmingly about distribution rather than trend. The trend-specific subset is a handful of papers, none at continental scale across a mammal fleet.
3. Camera-trap networks have not previously supported multi-species continental trend estimates. Prior work is single-site, with [Rich et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/geb.12600) the main multi-site exception, and it estimates occupancy and richness rather than trend.
4. The agency baseline is harvest-based and effort-confounded, which gives camera-anchored estimates clear comparative value even where their intervals are wide.

One counterweight is worth stating before a reviewer raises it. The bird literature has solved a version of this problem with better data, because eBird checklists carry effort and iNaturalist records do not. Our defence is the camera stream, and the paper should be explicit that the iNaturalist contribution is spatial coverage rather than temporal signal.
