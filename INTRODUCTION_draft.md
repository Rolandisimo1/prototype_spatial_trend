# Introduction

Conservation action depends on knowing which populations are declining and which are not. Monitoring provides that knowledge, and when it is designed to inform decisions rather than simply to accumulate data, it directs limited resources toward the species that need them ([Yoccoz et al. 2001](<https://doi.org/10.1016/s0169-5347(01)02205-4>), [Nichols et al. 2006](<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tree.2006.08.007>)). Poorly designed monitoring, however, consumes effort without detecting the changes it was meant to detect ([Legg et al. 2005](<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvman.2005.04.016>), [Lindenmayer et al. 2018](<https://doi.org/10.1071/9781486308934>)), and long-term datasets capable of resolving population change remain scarce ([Magurran et al. 2010](<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tree.2010.06.016>)). The need is urgent. Roughly one quarter of mammal species are threatened with extinction ([Schipper et al. 2008](<https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1165115>)), population sizes are falling even among species that remain common ([Dirzo et al. 2014](<https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1251817>), [Ceballos et al. 2017](<https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1704949114>)), and many vertebrates now persist as small remnant populations ([Ceballos et al. 2020](<https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1922686117>)).

Monitoring at the scale conservation requires is expensive, and for North American mammals it has largely not happened. State wildlife agencies estimate furbearer trends from catch-per-unit-effort in annual trapper surveys, supplemented by bowhunter observation and spotlight counts. These indices confound changes in animal populations with changes in trapper numbers and pelt prices, a limitation the agencies state explicitly in their own reports, and they cover only harvested species. Most of the North American mammal fauna therefore has no systematic trend estimate at all, which matches the broader finding that monitoring is inadequate for the majority of threatened vertebrates ([Scheele et al. 2019](<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biocon.2019.04.023>)).

Birds show what is possible when volunteers contribute at scale. Citizen science has become a standard tool in ecology ([Bonney et al. 2009](<https://doi.org/10.1525/bio.2009.59.11.9>), [Dickinson et al. 2010](<https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-ecolsys-102209-144636>)), and for birds it has produced datasets large enough to support continental inference ([Sullivan et al. 2013](<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biocon.2013.11.003>), [Hochachka et al. 2011](<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tree.2011.11.006>)). The North American Breeding Bird Survey has underpinned avian trend estimation for five decades ([E. et al. 2017](<https://doi.org/10.5066/f7c24tnp>)), and [Fink et al. 2019](<https://doi.org/10.1002/eap.2056>) extended trend estimation to eBird by modelling full annual cycle distribution from checklist data. These analyses have demonstrated a net loss of nearly three billion birds in North America since 1970 ([Rosenberg et al. 2019](<https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaw1313>)) and shown that declines fall most heavily on the species that are currently most abundant ([Johnston et al. 2025](<https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adn4381>)). Opportunistic bird records support trend estimation as well, provided the observation process is modelled ([Horns et al. 2018](<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biocon.2018.02.027>), [Walker et al. 2017](<https://doi.org/10.5751/ace-00960-120104>)), and such estimates have been validated against standardized surveys ([Feng et al. 2021](<https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0257226>), [Boersch‐Supan et al. 2019](<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biocon.2019.108286>)).

One feature of eBird makes this work. Checklists are semi-structured: observers report how long they searched, how far they travelled, which protocol they followed, and whether they recorded every species they detected ([Johnston et al. 2021](<https://doi.org/10.1111/ddi.13271>)). We can therefore measure effort directly, interpret non-detections, and model the observation process explicitly.

Mammals present a harder problem. Most species are nocturnal and secretive, and an observer walking a transect detects them far less often than birds. Opportunistic mammal records are nonetheless accumulating quickly, and iNaturalist now holds them at continental density ([Jensen et al. 2026](<https://doi.org/10.1093/jmammal/gyag025>), [Mason et al. 2025](<https://doi.org/10.1093/biosci/biaf104>)), with most mammal observations documented by photographs of the animal itself rather than of tracks or scat ([Alyetama et al. 2026](<https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0353282>)). However, iNaturalist records carry no effort field. An observation reports that someone photographed an animal; it does not report how long they searched, where else they went, or which animals they saw and chose not to photograph. We must therefore construct an effort measure from the records themselves ([Cecco et al. 2021](<https://doi.org/10.1093/biosci/biab093>), [Callaghan et al. 2026](<https://doi.org/10.1093/biosci/biag135>)), and we must do so knowing that unstructured records over-represent large-bodied and conspicuous species ([Callaghan et al. 2021](<https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-98584-7>)).

Methods for extracting trends from such records do exist, developed largely outside mammalogy. List-length analysis and occupancy models recover reliable distribution trends from opportunistic records when the observation process is modelled explicitly ([Szabo et al. 2010](<https://doi.org/10.1890/09-0877.1>), [van Strien et al. 2013](<https://doi.org/10.1111/1365-2664.12158>), [Isaac et al. 2014](<https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210x.12254>)), and opportunistic data already inform formal conservation assessments ([Maes et al. 2015](<https://doi.org/10.1111/bij.12530>)). The choice of response variable matters: species richness per unit effort trends artifactually negative as effort grows, while abundance per unit effort is considerably more robust ([Soberón et al. 2026](<https://doi.org/10.17161/bi.v20i1.24266>)). Butterflies provide the clearest non-avian demonstration that structured and unstructured counts can be combined to estimate abundance trends ([Dennis et al. 2017](<https://doi.org/10.1111/cobi.12956>), [Wepprich et al. 2019](<https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0216270>)), against a backdrop of well-documented decline ([Warren et al. 2021](<https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2002551117>)). Opportunistic photographs also support continental phenological trends ([Taylor et al. 2019](<https://doi.org/10.1101/794396>)), though phenology concerns within-year timing and so avoids the effort-drift problem that between-year abundance estimates must solve. Reviews of how community science data have been used find distribution modelling far better developed than trend estimation ([Feldman et al. 2021](<https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0234587>), [Mesaglio et al. 2021](<https://doi.org/10.1071/wr20154>)).

Camera traps supply the structured mammal data that opportunistic records lack. Camera trapping is now standard for estimating occupancy and density ([Burton et al. 2015](<https://doi.org/10.1111/1365-2664.12432>)), supported by hierarchical models that separate detection from occurrence ([MacKenzie et al. 2002](<https://doi.org/10.1890/0012-9658(2002)083[2248:esorwd]2.0.co;2>), [Royle et al. 2003](<https://doi.org/10.1890/0012-9658(2003)084[0777:eafrpa]2.0.co;2>)) and by spatially explicit methods for unmarked populations ([Chandler et al. 2013](<https://doi.org/10.1214/12-aoas610>)). Raw detection frequencies are a poor substitute for these models, because detection and abundance are confounded ([Sollmann et al. 2013](<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biocon.2012.12.025>)). Coordinated networks have scaled camera trapping to national extents ([Steenweg et al. 2016](<https://doi.org/10.1002/fee.1448>)): Snapshot USA has run annual standardized surveys across the United States since 2019 ([Cove et al. 2021](<https://doi.org/10.1002/ecy.3353>), [Kays et al. 2022](<https://doi.org/10.1002/ecy.3775>)), an approach now replicated internationally through Snapshot Global, including national monitoring of 35 protected areas in Chile, and shared infrastructure supports processing the resulting imagery ([Ahumada et al. 2019](<https://doi.org/10.1017/s0376892919000298>)). These networks have supported continental multi-species inference about occupancy and richness ([Rich et al. 2017](<https://doi.org/10.1111/geb.12600>), [Burton et al. 2024](<https://doi.org/10.1038/s41559-024-02363-2>)), and in the tropics the TEAM network demonstrated standardized multi-species trend assessment within protected areas ([Beaudrot et al. 2016](<https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.1002357>)). Even the largest camera networks, however, sample a small fraction of a continent, and detections of sparse species are few. Power to detect a trend from cameras alone is consequently limited for most species ([Norman et al. 2026](<https://doi.org/10.32942/x28x0d>)).

The two data streams have complementary weaknesses, which makes combining them attractive. Cameras measure effort precisely and produce interpretable non-detections, but cover little ground. iNaturalist covers the continent but measures no effort. Integrating data of different types within a single model is a well-developed statistical enterprise built on joint likelihoods, in which each data type informs shared ecological parameters while retaining its own observation model ([Fithian et al. 2014](<https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210x.12242>), [Pacifici et al. 2016](<https://doi.org/10.1002/ecy.1710>), [Miller et al. 2019](<https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210x.13110>), [Fletcher et al. 2019](<https://doi.org/10.1002/ecy.2710>), [Isaac et al. 2019](<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tree.2019.08.006>)). Integration usually improves precision, though not in every configuration ([Simmonds et al. 2020](<https://doi.org/10.1111/ecog.05146>)). Applications to population dynamics and trend remain far rarer than applications to distribution ([Schaub et al. 2010](<https://doi.org/10.1007/s10336-010-0632-7>), [Zipkin et al. 2017](<https://doi.org/10.1002/ecy.1831>), [Pagel et al. 2014](<https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210x.12221>), [Grüss et al. 2019](<https://doi.org/10.1093/icesjms/fsz075>), [Doser et al. 2022](<https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210x.13811>), [Zipkin et al. 2021](<https://doi.org/10.1002/fee.2290>)).

Several data streams have been brought to bear on North American mammal status before. For example, [Jachowski et al. 2021](<https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0254387>) assessed three weasel species using harvest records, museum specimens, iNaturalist observations and a national camera survey, examining each stream in turn. That study compares sources rather than fitting them together, and the step of combining camera and opportunistic data within one model was taken by [Goldstein et al. 2025](<https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.01.17.633640>), who fitted camera-trap detections and iNaturalist records jointly for North American mammals. They treated cameras as the reference stream and estimated a parameter measuring how closely the two corresponded, and they found that the streams agreed: iNaturalist records and camera detections described the same ecological signal and the same spatial patterns. Their model was fitted to a single period and included no temporal trend.

Adding a time axis to that model requires testing an assumption it would otherwise inherit. Agreement in space does not guarantee agreement in time. The processes that generate iNaturalist records change from year to year for reasons unrelated to animal populations, because participants join, smartphone cameras improve, and attention shifts among taxa. A data stream can therefore locate animals correctly in space while misreporting how their numbers change.

Here we add a temporal dimension to the integrated camera-trap and iNaturalist framework and apply it to a fleet of North American mammals, asking both what the population trends are and which data stream to believe when the two disagree. We fit each species' camera and iNaturalist data to a shared spatial surface carrying a national trend, and we decompose that trend into a camera-anchored component, estimated from survey data with measured effort, and an increment identified only by the iNaturalist stream. We test two competing predictions. If iNaturalist records carry population signal through time, the two components should agree in sign and magnitude and integration should mainly improve precision. If instead the temporal signal in iNaturalist reflects observer dynamics, the two components should diverge even though the streams agree in space, and deciding which quantity to report then becomes a substantive result.

We fitted these models to 26,517 camera deployments from 60 projects, totalling 966,225 survey nights, together with 1,053,349 research-grade iNaturalist records, spanning 2008 to 2025. The second stream is large but uneven in time: iNaturalist mammal records within our study area grew from 2,798 in 2008 to 369,355 in 2025, a 130-fold increase over the same period in which we estimate population change. Fitting a fleet of species rather than one lets us ask which species can be monitored this way at all, a question no single-species analysis can answer. We find that the two streams diverge in time for a substantial minority of species. For bobcat (*Lynx rufus*), northern raccoon (*Procyon lotor*), American black bear (*Ursus americanus*), red fox (*Vulpes vulpes*), coyote (*Canis latrans*) and mule deer (*Odocoileus hemionus*), the camera-anchored and iNaturalist-identified trends carry opposite signs, and reporting the iNaturalist quantity would have reversed our conclusion for each.
